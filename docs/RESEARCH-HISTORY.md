# The period, distilled

Research notes on real naval combat, 1600–1720 — the buccaneer and privateer
era, before ships of the line. Kept here because most of the game's rules came
out of these facts, and future rules should too. Each section ends with the
design hook it suggests.

## Ships almost never sank in battle

Wood floats. Fights ended by **striking colors** (surrender), **boarding**,
or — the great terror — **fire reaching the magazine**. Battering a ship to
pieces was slow and wasted the prize. A sunk ship paid nothing: prizes had to
be sailed into port and adjudicated by an admiralty court before anyone saw
money. Privateer crews were paid almost entirely in prize shares.

*Hook: victory is breaking the enemy's ability or will to fight. The economy
should always prefer capture over destruction — sinking her is losing money.*

## Three ways to disable a ship

Whole navies disagreed about which to aim for, which means it is a real choice
with no dominant answer:

- **Hull** — English doctrine: fire on the downroll, smash timbers, kill crews
  with the splinter storm (splinters, not balls, caused most casualties).
- **Rigging** — French doctrine: fire on the uproll with chain and bar shot,
  immobilize her, take her intact and valuable.
- **Crew** — grapeshot to sweep the deck, always just before boarding.

*Hook: hull / rigging / crew as independent damage tracks, with shot type as
the aiming decision. (Already in the game.)*

## The weather gage is a trade-off, not a buff

The windward ship dictates whether, when, and how the fight happens — she can
bear down at a moment of her choosing, or decline entirely. But the leeward
ship can always disengage by running downwind, and in heavy weather the
windward ship heels toward the enemy so hard her lower gunports can flood.
Aggressor versus escape artist, encoded in geometry. A wind shift mid-fight
could flip the gage entirely.

*Hook: never make windward strictly better. The leeward player's compensation
is the exit.*

## Rig is identity

Fore-and-aft rigs (sloops, schooners) point to ~45° off the wind and tack
reliably; square rigs manage ~60–70°, make more leeway, and risk hanging in
irons when they tack — which is why big ships usually wore around the long way
instead. Counter-intuitive but true: dead downwind is *slower* than a broad
reach for most ships, because the hull and forward sails blanket the after
sails and apparent wind drops.

This is the whole reason pirates sailed sloops: they could out-point anything
sent to catch them, and their shallow draught let them run over banks and into
inlets where a frigate dared not follow. Small fore-and-aft craft also needed
far fewer hands to sail, which left more hands free to fight.

*Hook: per-class speed tables across the points of sail do more for ship
feel than any stat. Draught as terrain interaction. (Both in the game.)*

## Gunnery was short-ranged and rhythmic

No sights, no fire control. Culverins and demi-culverins could throw a ball
half a mile, but real fights closed to a few hundred yards, often to "pistol
shot," because accuracy collapsed with distance. A drilled crew fired a
broadside roughly every two to three minutes, and the rate degraded as gunners
fell. Raking — firing down the length of a ship through her bow or, worse,
her stern — sent every ball the full length of the deck; a stern rake could
also smash the rudder.

Shot types: round (hull), chain and bar (rigging), grape (crew, close),
double-shotting (two balls, devastating and inaccurate, close only), heated
shot (rare at sea — too dangerous to the firer; a shore-battery weapon).

*Hook: short ranges force the closing decision. Reload rhythm makes WHEN to
fire as important as at what. (Hit chance by range is now in the game, as is
the gale heel that shuts the lee ports.) Heated shot, if ever added, should threaten the
ship that fires it. (Bennett's red-hot-shot self-risk idea matches history.)*

## The engagement had an arc

Sighting → identification (rig and flag read at a distance) → a chase that
could last hours or days, decided by sail trim and lucky chase-gun hits on
rigging → jockeying for the gage → broadsides at short range → a disabling
phase → boarding, surrender, or escape. Flying false colors to approach was a
legal ruse of war — but only until you fired; you had to hoist true colors
before engaging. Fighting under a false flag was perfidy.

*Hook: the arc is a scenario template. Acts of it (a pure chase, a pure
gunnery duel) make good single levels; the campaign can compose them. False
colors is a ready-made mechanic with a built-in rules-of-war edge.*

## Crews, captains and mutiny

Pirate captains were elected and could be deposed by the crew; morale was job
security. Quartermasters counterbalanced captains. Crews signed articles
setting shares and compensation for wounds. A letter of marque was the entire
legal difference between a privateer (paid, ranked, welcome in port) and a
pirate (hanged if caught) — and captains slid between the two when wars ended
faster than news traveled.

*Hook: crew morale as a track the player manages with real decisions, not a
bar that fills. The marque-versus-piracy line is the campaign's spine.*

## Ship types of the era, for the data file

| Type | Rig | Guns | Character |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bermuda sloop | fore-and-aft | 4–12 light | fast, weatherly, shallow; the pirate favorite |
| Schooner | fore-and-aft | light | even fewer hands, fragile, superb to windward |
| Brigantine | square fore / f-a main | ~10 | the step up: endurance over agility |
| Pinnace | full-rigged, small | 5–16 | fast armed-merchant scout |
| Fluyt | Dutch cargo, minimal crew | few | slow, cheap, THE fat prize |
| Guarda costa / light frigate | square | 12–20 | the hunter the player dodges early and becomes later |
| Galleon | square, high-sided | 20–60 | slow treasure fortress; a campaign finale, not an encounter |

*Hook: the era caps naturally at small ships — every gun and hand matters.
Ships of the line are a sequel, and the rating system is the career ladder.*
