# Prior art, distilled

What fifty years of age-of-sail games got right and wrong. Mechanics research
across the landmark tabletop systems (from the primary rulebooks) and the
notable video games. Kept short; the lesson matters more than the citation.

## The tabletop landmarks

**Wooden Ships & Iron Men (Avalon Hill, 1975).** Secret simultaneous plotted
movement — both sides write orders blind, reveal together — praised for
decades as "as near to perfection as one could hope." Four damage tracks
(hull/guns/crew/rigging); crew quality (elite→poor) quietly modifies nearly
every roll, which is the cheapest depth in the whole genre. Died of paperwork:
per-ship log sheets and an opaque rulebook. *We keep: blind orders, crew
quality as a universal modifier. We are spared: the bookkeeping — the
computer keeps the log.*

**Sails of Glory (Ares, 2013).** Wind attitude read at a glance from
color-coded bands on the ship base; damage drawn from counter bags (fully
diceless); each broadside reloads independently so batteries stagger.
Plan-one-turn-ahead card commitment. Criticized: fiddly component overhead,
and the basic mode was too shallow to be fun — depth had to be layered in.
*We keep: glanceable attitude, per-side reload stagger. Warning: don't ship
the shallow mode as the game.*

**Flying Colors (GMT, 2005).** The cleanest movement-allowance-by-attitude
table (fixed hexes per point of sail); tacking as a risk roll that can leave
you in irons; rake multipliers — bow ×1.5, stern ×2; striking driven by
damage plus morale rather than hit points reaching zero. Command-and-control
activation solved fleet scale, which we don't need yet but squadron play
(roadmap 13) should study. *We keep: the attitude table shape, tack risk,
rake math, strike checks — all already in the engine.*

**Oak & Iron (Firelock, 2019).** Two clean resources — hull damage and crew
fatigue — with a deliberate split: shooting cripples the ship, boarding
breaks the crew. Fast, teachable, well-loved. Its sins: the wind never shifts
in the core rules (direction only changes by scenario event card), and
boarding math favors the attacker so heavily that small ships eat capital
ships. *We keep: the shoot-to-cripple / board-to-break framing. We avoid:
static wind, attacker-biased boarding.*

## The video games

**Sid Meier's Pirates! (2004).** Its genius was never combat — it was choices
chaining into legible consequences (rank → invitation → gift → duel →
marriage, each step earned by the last) and one itemized score across
independent activity threads. Combat is a delivery mechanism for
consequences. Per-nation reputation with concrete perks. *The campaign's DNA.*

**Naval Action.** The deepest sailing model ever shipped, and the single
most-praised realism lever is per-ship polar curves — a schooner and a
frigate feel different at every point of sail. Forcing an enemy into irons to
set up boarding is a real documented tactic. *Validates the per-class speeds
table; argues for widening class differences as ships are added.*

**Ultimate Admiral: Age of Sail.** Ablative armor by facing makes raking a
positioning reward; fire and flooding compete for the same damage-control
crew (forced triage — a great shape for damage-control orders, roadmap 8);
surrendered ships must still be physically boarded to secure. Its campaign
was criticized as scripted-puzzle pacing. *Steal the triage; avoid the
puzzle-campaign.*

**Sunless Sea.** The template for pressure without combat: a few gauges
(fuel, supplies, terror) make distance-from-safety itself the antagonist, and
the legacy system makes loss feel like accumulated knowledge. Also a warning:
its own postmortem admits a roguelike/story identity crisis — decide which
failure model the campaign uses and commit. *The reference for the
between-battles layer.*

**Tortuga (2023) — the cautionary tale.** Turn-based, wind-driven, momentum
movement: exactly our pitch. Reviewers called its fights "a chore" — huge
turning radii turned realism into waiting. *The anti-toil test: sluggishness
must create commitment tension, never dead turns. If a mechanic makes the
player wait rather than decide, cut it.*

## Cross-cutting lessons

1. **Damage models converge on 2–3 tracks** (hull / rigging / crew-morale)
   with shot type as the aim. Everyone lands here; we already did.
2. **The two proven anti-twitch turn structures** are blind simultaneous
   orders (ours) and command-based activation (fleet scale, later).
3. **Boarding is the genre's universally under-baked subsystem** — almost
   every game reduced it to a coin flip or a dead mini-game. The texture that
   worked: positional pushing with environmental kills (Rogue Waters),
   escalating wounds with a back-off option (Blackbeard), and an instant
   pre-resolve when the odds are lopsided (Pirates!). Bennett's running fight
   for the deck is already ahead of most of the field.
4. **Crew quality as a universal modifier** is the cheapest depth available.
5. **Depth died of bookkeeping in every deep tabletop game.** A computer
   game gets that depth for free — but UI clutter is our version of the
   log sheet. Every mechanic must earn its pixels.
